# Oil 101 Episode 10 Source Packet

## Episode contract

- Title: 储罐是市场的时间机器
- Central question: 今天多出来的油去了哪里，为什么几个月后还会回来影响市场？
- Primary sources: Oil 101 Chapter 12 Storage and Chapter 13 Seasonality.
- Editorial mode: system-narrative.
- Story rail: 一批原油在炼厂春季检修时抵达终端，先进入库存，随后被库存报告看见，再在夏季炼厂恢复运行时被提取。
- Memory images: 储罐水位、四层日历、接近罐顶时越来越窄的入口。
- Spoken-number policy: 正文不用阿拉伯数字，不做心算；精确数字仅留在本资料包。
- Exclusions: 不展开期货曲线、负油价事件、战略储备国家排名、储罐设备百科和完整天气案例。

## Source claims

### [S12.WHY] Storage bridges mismatched timing

Production, shipping, refining and consumption do not match minute by minute. Storage acts as a shock absorber between continuous or lumpy flows. A barrel may spend time in a tank, cavern, pipeline or ship between the reservoir and the final consumer.

The useful interpretation for this episode is temporal rather than architectural: inventory moves a current surplus into a later period, or releases an earlier surplus into the present. Storage does not manufacture petroleum.

Source: Chapter 12, `Why Store Oil?`

### [S12.MOTIVES] Three motives must not be confused

Oil can be held for operational, strategic, or commercial reasons.

- Operational inventory buffers delivery schedules, refinery runs and product lifting.
- Strategic reserves are government emergency stocks.
- Commercial storage can be held when later prices cover the complete cost of carrying the barrel.

The episode follows operational inventory. Strategic reserves and commercial curve trades appear only as boundaries so that a listener does not treat every stock build as the same decision.

Source: Chapter 12, `Why Store Oil?`

### [S12.CATEGORIES] Storage locations perform different jobs

Refinery tankage, terminal tanks, pipeline fill, tank-farm hubs, salt caverns, floating storage and strategic reserves differ in accessibility, turnover, cost and purpose. Pipeline fill is real inventory required to keep a line operating and cannot be casually withdrawn. Salt caverns suit large-volume storage. A tanker may become temporary storage when timing or trade conditions delay discharge.

The episode groups these forms by the question they answer: Is the barrel immediately accessible, in the right place, of the right quality and connected to the required outlet?

Source: Chapter 12, `Categories of Storage` and `Salt Caverns`.

### [S12.WORKING] Working capacity is not shell capacity

Every tank has an unusable residual or heel at the bottom. Industry working-capacity figures exclude this dead stock. A tank reported near full is near the limit of usable capacity, not merely a shell with an evenly available empty slice.

As available working space shrinks, the next incoming barrel becomes harder to place. Location, tank rights, pipeline direction, quality segregation and withdrawal capability can tighten before an aggregate headline reaches a theoretical maximum.

Source: Chapter 12, `Tank Types`.

### [S12.HUB] Inventory is local before it is global

The Cushing example shows why inflow and outflow connections matter. When inflows exceed the hub's ability to move crude onward, stocks build and local pricing can weaken. A barrel elsewhere does not automatically solve a capacity problem at the delivery location.

The episode uses the general mechanism and does not retell the negative-price event.

Source: Chapter 12, `Cushing, Oklahoma: The Pipeline Crossroads`.

### [S12.CARRY] Future prices may pay for time

Commercial storage is economic only when the price available for later delivery exceeds the full cost of holding the barrel, including storage, insurance, financing and physical losses. The market tends to use cheaper capacity before expensive capacity.

The episode gives this only as a preview. The futures-curve mechanics belong to a later episode.

Source: Chapter 12, `The Contango Storage Play`.

### [S12.REPORT] Inventory reports need a reference frame

Weekly reports separate crude and major products and also distinguish important regions and hubs. The common market frame compares the current stock path with a five-year range. A single weekly change is not self-explanatory; the product, location, seasonal baseline and operational cause matter.

Source: Chapter 12, `Inventory Reports and the Five-Year Range`.

### [S13.CALENDARS] Four calendars overlap

The oil year contains overlapping driving, heating, refinery-maintenance and weather calendars. Driving demand is strongest in summer. Heating demand is strongest in winter. Refineries schedule maintenance in the shoulder periods between product-demand peaks. Atlantic hurricane risk overlaps late driving season and fall maintenance.

Source: Chapter 13, `Four Overlapping Calendars`.

### [S13.PRODUCTS] Product demand changes the refinery's job

Gasoline demand rises into the summer driving season. Heating oil and propane rise in cold weather. Seasonal fuel specifications also change what can legally and economically enter a market. The episode does not repeat the detailed blending explanation from Episode 07; it uses only the consequence that refineries and terminals must prepare the right product before demand arrives.

Source: Chapter 13, `Gasoline: Memorial Day to Labor Day`, `Summer Grade and Winter Grade`, and `Heating Oil, Propane, and the HDD Framework`.

### [S13.TURNAROUND] Maintenance can build crude while tightening products

Refineries tend to run hardest during major product-demand peaks and schedule maintenance in spring and fall shoulder periods. During a turnaround, crude throughput falls. Crude can therefore accumulate upstream of the refinery even while product supply becomes tighter downstream.

This is the episode's main correction to the idea that every crude-stock build means final demand collapsed.

Source: Chapter 13, `The Refinery Turnaround Calendar`.

### [S13.WEATHER] Weather can strike different links

Hurricanes can disrupt offshore production, ports, refineries and pipelines. The market consequence depends on which side is hit. A production outage can tighten crude supply; a refinery outage can reduce crude demand while also tightening refined products.

The episode keeps this as a diagnostic boundary and does not narrate individual storms.

Source: Chapter 13, `Atlantic Hurricanes`.

### [S13.CANCEL] Crude is less seasonal than its products

Gasoline peaks in summer, heating fuels peak in winter and refinery maintenance lowers crude runs in shoulder months. When these effects are aggregated at the crude level, much of the product seasonality offsets. Crude therefore displays flatter seasonality than individual products.

Source: Chapter 13, `Why Crude Oil Itself Is Less Seasonal Than Its Products`.

### [S13.BASELINE] Seasonality is a baseline, not a forecast

The seasonal calendar describes what a normal year tends to do. Current weather, outages, logistics and demand determine how the present year departs from that baseline. A stock move becomes informative only after this normal pattern is considered.

Source: Chapter 13, `Why Crude Oil Itself Is Less Seasonal Than Its Products`.

## Show-note precision excluded from dialogue

- Chapter 12 lists typical refinery operating tankage as several days to several weeks of crude runs, but the spoken episode does not ask the listener to retain a number.
- The five-year range is a week-by-week historical comparison band, not a guarantee that values inside it are safe or values outside it are causal proof.
- Chapter 13 provides monthly gasoline-demand and refinery-utilization tables. The spoken episode preserves direction and timing rather than reciting the table.
- Strategic-stock levels and dated political examples are intentionally excluded because they change and are not needed for the episode's core mechanism.
